# Frameworks

A framework is a scaffolding for a page's structure, not a formula to follow
mechanically. Pick one deliberately based on the reader's stage of awareness
(Schwartz — below) and how strong the offer is. Name the chosen framework in
draft-mode output.

If no framework fits the page, write plainly and say so. A forced framework
reads worse than a well-organised argument.

---

## Schwartz's Five Stages of Awareness

The most useful single lens. From Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*
(1966). Every framework below reads better when it starts from the reader's
stage.

1. **Unaware** — reader doesn't know they have the problem. Copy has to open
   with a story or a startling fact. Rare for web copy — usually cheaper to
   target readers already at stage 2+.
2. **Problem-aware** — reader knows the problem, doesn't know solutions exist.
   Lead with the pain, name it precisely, then introduce the category of
   solution. PAS works well here.
3. **Solution-aware** — reader knows solutions exist, doesn't know yours. Lead
   with the category, then differentiate. BAB and StoryBrand work well here.
4. **Product-aware** — reader knows your product, hasn't bought. Lead with
   proof, objections, and specifics. Case studies, guarantees, comparisons.
5. **Most aware** — reader is ready to buy, wants the offer. Lead with price,
   terms, and CTA. Don't re-sell the problem.

**Rule:** never sell the problem to someone already sold on the solution.

## AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

The oldest framework in copy (attributed to E. St. Elmo Lewis, 1898). Best for
top-of-funnel landing pages where you have one shot at the reader.

- **A** — hook their attention. Headline that names the specific pain or the
  specific outcome.
- **I** — build interest. What is this, and why should they care in the next
  10 seconds?
- **D** — build desire. Specific benefits, concrete proof, contrast with the
  status quo.
- **A** — action. One clear CTA, friction acknowledged.

*Best for:* stage 2 / 3 readers, cold traffic from ads.

Example H1/subhead/CTA cluster:
```
H1:    Stop chasing invoices at the weekend.
Sub:   Acme sends the reminders for you, in your tone of voice.
CTA:   Try it free — no card
```

## PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solution

Direct-response classic. Works when the reader is problem-aware but hasn't
acted on the pain.

- **P** — name the problem in the reader's own words.
- **A** — twist the knife. What does the problem *cost* — money, time,
  reputation, sleep? Concrete numbers if possible.
- **S** — the solution, positioned as the natural response.

*Best for:* stage 2 readers; consultancy, coaching, agencies, B2B services.

Risk: over-agitation reads as manipulation. If you can't say the pain in one
sentence without sounding like a late-night infomercial, drop the framework.

---

## PASTOR — Person, Ache, Story, Testimony, Offer, Response

From John Meese and Ray Edwards. A longer-form extension of PAS, better for
sales pages and long emails than for a hero section.

- **P** — Person. Who is the reader, what do they care about?
- **A** — Ache. Their pain, in specific terms.
- **S** — Story / Solution. How your product / method resolves it.
- **T** — Testimony. Named proof, specific results.
- **O** — Offer. What they get, at what price, on what terms.
- **R** — Response. What to do next, single CTA.

*Best for:* long sales pages, webinar registration pages, cold email flows.

---

## 4Ps — Promise, Picture, Proof, Push

From Henry Hoke Sr. and popularised by Ray Edwards. Compact enough for a hero
section, complete enough for a whole page.

- **Promise** — the outcome you'll deliver, headline-sized.
- **Picture** — what life looks like with the promise fulfilled (concrete,
  sensory).
- **Proof** — evidence the promise holds (numbers, names, guarantees).
- **Push** — the CTA plus a nudge (scarcity, deadline, or reciprocity).

*Best for:* SaaS home pages, feature pages, campaign landers.

---

## FAB — Features, Advantages, Benefits

The oldest B2B framework. Reorders the natural writing instinct.

- **F** — Feature. What the product has or does. (`AES-256 encryption`)
- **A** — Advantage. What that means technically. (`Data is unreadable if
  intercepted.`)
- **B** — Benefit. What that means to the reader. (`Client data stays private,
  even if a laptop is lost.`)

**Rule:** always write the benefit first in the copy, even though FAB is
described in the F-A-B order. Readers scan for benefits; features are proof.

*Best for:* feature pages, comparison pages, technical products where features
matter but the reader is not technical.

---

## Before-After-Bridge (BAB)

Compact and effective for hero sections and cold email.

- **Before** — the reader's current state, painful specifics.
- **After** — the reader's desired state, painful specifics.
- **Bridge** — the product as the way to get there.

*Best for:* stage 3 readers, transformation-driven products (fitness,
finance, productivity, education).

Example:
```
Before:  Payroll takes you a full day every month, and you still get
         corrections from HMRC.
After:   Payroll takes 20 minutes and files itself.
Bridge:  Acme Payroll — HMRC-recognised, £12/employee/month.
```

## StoryBrand SB7

From Donald Miller, *Building a StoryBrand*. Casts the reader as the hero and
the brand as the guide.

1. A **character** (the reader) has a…
2. …**problem** (external, internal, and philosophical). They meet…
3. …a **guide** (the brand) who gives them…
4. …a **plan** (usually three steps), and…
5. …**calls them to action**, which helps them avoid…
6. …**failure**, and end in…
7. …**success**.

*Best for:* home pages that need to reset a confused audience; brands with a
strong point of view; service businesses.

**Rule of thumb — the brand is the guide, not the hero.** Copy that opens with
"We are a leading provider of…" fails the framework in the first sentence.

## Sugarman's Slippery Slide

From Joseph Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*. Not a page structure —
a principle for sentence-to-sentence flow.

Every sentence's job is to make the reader read the next sentence. The first
sentence should be very short. Openings that pull well:

- A question the reader already asks themselves.
- A startling fact.
- A concrete image.
- A short declarative that implies a story.

Once the reader is sliding, don't stop them. No throat-clearing paragraphs, no
sub-heads that give away the punchline early, no long sentences at natural
break-points.

*Best for:* long-form sales pages, blog intros, cold email openers.

## Cialdini's Seven Principles of Persuasion

Not a framework — a checklist of levers. Use at most one per section; overuse
reads as manipulation.

1. **Reciprocity** — give something first (free trial, free guide, free audit).
2. **Commitment & Consistency** — small yes leads to bigger yes (quiz → email →
   demo → sale).
3. **Social Proof** — named customers, specific numbers, third-party ratings.
4. **Authority** — credentials, awards, press mentions, industry certifications.
5. **Liking** — the brand feels like a peer (tone, imagery, story).
6. **Scarcity** — limited spots, limited time, limited stock — only if true.
7. **Unity** — shared identity (`for freelancers, by freelancers`).

Never fake scarcity. Fake countdown timers are a trust killer and are
increasingly caught by consumer-protection regulators.

*Source:* Robert Cialdini, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (new &
expanded edition, 2021).

## Choosing a framework

| Reader stage | Page type | Framework |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Blog / SEO landing | Sugarman opener → PAS body |
| Problem-aware | Landing page from cold ad | AIDA or PAS |
| Solution-aware | Home page, category page | 4Ps or BAB |
| Solution-aware | Home page with brand story | StoryBrand SB7 |
| Product-aware | Feature page | FAB (benefits first) |
| Product-aware | Sales page | PASTOR |
| Most aware | Pricing page, checkout | Skip frameworks — offer + proof + CTA |

If the reader stage is uncertain, ask the user. It's the single input that
changes the framework choice most.
